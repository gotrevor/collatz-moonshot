# Knight (2026): Collatz high cycles do not exist

## Citation

Kevin Knight, “Collatz high cycles do not exist,” *Discrete Mathematics* 349 (2026), article 114812. DOI: [10.1016/j.disc.2025.114812](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.disc.2025.114812).

The article was accepted and first published online in September 2025, then assigned to the March 2026 journal issue. That is why its DOI and publisher identifier contain `2025` while the volume is dated 2026.

## Elevator pitch

Encode a closed Collatz trajectory by its binary odd/even pattern. Among all rational cycles with a fixed length and number of odd terms, the “high cycle” is generated by the most evenly balanced pattern—an upper Christoffel word. Knight exploits the symmetry between this word and its reversal to produce two members of the same cycle whose difference could be integral only if the odd denominator \(2^k-3^x\) divided a power of two. That forces the denominator to equal one, which yields only the trivial \(1\leftrightarrow2\) cycle. Thus no nontrivial high cycle can consist entirely of positive integers.

## Executive summary

The paper studies the shortcut Collatz map

\[
T(n)=
\begin{cases}
(3n+1)/2, & n\text{ odd},\\
n/2, & n\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
\]

It extends \(T\) from integers to rational numbers with odd denominators. This larger domain contains many rational cycles. Each cycle can be encoded by a parity vector: a binary word of length \(k\), containing \(x\) ones, where `1` denotes an odd step and `0` an even step.

For fixed \((k,x)\), Knight focuses on two extremal rational cycles:

- the **circuit**, whose smallest member is the smallest among all \((k,x)\)-cycles; and
- the **high cycle**, whose smallest member is the largest among all \((k,x)\)-cycles.

Steiner had already proved that no nontrivial circuit consists of positive integers. Knight’s main theorem proves the analogous statement for high cycles.

## Parity vectors and rational cycles

If a parity vector \(v\) has ones at zero-based positions

\[
d_0(v)<d_1(v)<\cdots<d_{x-1}(v),
\]

then its associated rational cycle member is

\[
f(v)=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{x-1}2^{d_i(v)}3^{x-i-1}}
     {2^k-3^x}.
\]

Rotating \(v\) moves to another member of the same rational cycle. Positive cycles require \(2^k-3^x>0\), equivalently \(k>x\log_2 3\). The paper restricts to coprime \(k\) and \(x\), which makes the parity word—and hence the cycle—aperiodic.

The circuit uses the word

\[
v_c=1^x0^{k-x},
\]

which places all odd steps together. Knight includes a streamlined reproof of Steiner’s no-integer-circuit theorem. That proof still relies on deep lower bounds for \(2^k-3^x\) derived from Baker-style transcendence theory.

## Characterizing the high cycle

The high-cycle word \(v_h\) places its ones at positions

\[
\left\lfloor\frac{k}{x}i\right\rfloor,
\qquad 0\le i<x.
\]

This is the upper Christoffel word of slope \(x/k\), distributing odd steps as evenly as possible. For example, \((k,x)=(8,5)\) gives

\[
v_h=11011010.
\]

Earlier results of Halbeisen and Hungerbühler imply that \(f(v_h)\) is the smallest member of its own cycle, while also being maximal among the smallest members of all \((k,x)\)-cycles. Knight further shows that when \(k=\lceil x\log_2 3\rceil\), it is the highest such minimum across all positive cycle lengths having \(x\) odd terms.

## Main proof

An aperiodic upper Christoffel word has the form

\[
v_h=1u0,
\]

and its reversal is

\[
v_h^R=0u1.
\]

The reversal is also a rotation of \(v_h\), so both words describe the same rational cycle. Rotating each word once gives two more members encoded by \(u01\) and \(u10\). With

\[
D=2^k-3^x,
\]

their values are

\[
f(u01)=\frac{3g(u)+2^{k-1}}{D},
\qquad
f(u10)=\frac{3g(u)+2^{k-2}}{D}.
\]

If the high cycle consisted entirely of integers, both values would be integers, so their difference

\[
f(u01)-f(u10)=\frac{2^{k-2}}{D}
\]

would also be an integer. But \(D=2^k-3^x\) is positive and odd. The only positive odd divisor of a power of two is one, so \(D=1\). The equation

\[
2^k-3^x=1
\]

has no solution with \(x>0\) except \((k,x)=(2,1)\), which is the trivial cycle \(1\leftrightarrow2\). Therefore every nontrivial high cycle contains at least one non-integer member.

Unlike the no-circuit proof, this argument needs neither a closed form for the high-cycle member nor a deep lower bound on \(2^k-3^x\).

## Scope and limitations

- The result eliminates one distinguished extremal rational cycle for each coprime pair \((k,x)\).
- It does **not** eliminate all parity vectors or all possible positive-integer Collatz cycles.
- It therefore does not prove the weak Collatz conjecture, much less the full conjecture.
- It is a structural exclusion theorem, not an improvement to Eliahou’s numerical cycle-length bound.
- The proof concerns positive cycles; negative rational and integer cycles are outside its scope.

## Relation to the Eliahou paper

This is a separate paper, not a replacement or altered copy of Eliahou’s 1993 article. The two publisher identifiers are different:

- Eliahou (1993): `0012365X9390052U`, DOI `10.1016/0012-365X(93)90052-U`;
- Knight (2026): `S0012365X25004200`, DOI `10.1016/j.disc.2025.114812`.

Knight cites Eliahou for the fact that any hypothetical nontrivial integer cycle must contain very many terms, but Knight’s main contribution is the Christoffel-word integrality obstruction above.
